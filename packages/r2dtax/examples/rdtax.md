| The ability to reliably quantify skill levels and skill development presents a major area of technical uncertainty across the education technology industry. Practera is undertaking research into extending the analytic capability of its experiential learning platform through new machine learning (ML) techniques and methods for data mining. These new ML solutions aim to facilitate robust, real-time learning analytics (LA) to accurately predict learning outcomes and trigger personalised learning interventions that no other LA platform possess. Work to date on this activity has focused on:  Development of a novel method using temporal motifs and dynamic social network analysis (SNA) to understand team cohesion. Development of a new unsupervised machine learning methodology, that blends measurement science and ML methods, to identify students’ learning strategies from complex datasets.  Development of a novel tool to provide early warning of students at risk of failing based on learning platform engagement. Following initial development of the learning analytics (LA) tools above, work in FY23 focused on integrating and trialling the tools developed into the LA toolbox and incorporating novel privacy preserving analytics previously developed as part of a CRC-P project. Collection of data, feedback from students and monitoring of student engagement has continued and is required to inform further technology development and refinement.  Experiential learning programs generate vast amounts of heterogenous data as a result of diverse program structures, assessment types and skill taxonomies. This presents significant technical barriers to creating consistent, comparable skill development measures across the different learning experiences. Significant technical uncertainty remains in relation to achieving meaningful and accurate comparison of skills development.  Practera hypothesises that a novel approach to data normalisation and comparison, incorporating domain-specific adaptations to machine learning techniques, will enable meaningful cross-program skill development comparisons with sufficient accuracy to detect statistically significant differences in quantified skill matrices across different experiential learning program designs. Work in FY24 also focused on: Developing novel methods to normalise data collected (skill development measurements) from different experiential learning programs; and Development of a new ML enabled comparison tool for cross-program skill development comparisons. The complexities and technical uncertainties associated with capturing and measuring of competencies mean that successful development of a novel data normalisation and comparison technique may not be possible or result in different conclusions being drawn when comparing different learning program designs.   |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

Applying standard normalisation techniques (Z-scores, Min-Max) failed to produce meaningful comparisons. ANOVA tests on normalised scores frequently showed statistically insignificant differences (p \> 0.10) between program groups where skill development differences were strongly expected based on program design (e.g., high-intensity coaching vs. minimal guidance). Furthermore, rankings derived from normalised scores often lacked face validity when reviewed by experts, demonstrating these methods were inadequate for the data's heterogeneity.** **Experiment 4b (Influence Factors): Evaluation using regression models identified 'assessor type' (self vs. peer vs. expert) and 'rubric specificity' as highly significant (p \< 0.01) predictors of score variance, often explaining more variance than program structure itself. This quantitatively confirmed that assessment inconsistency was a primary barrier.** **Experiment 4c (Novel Models): Evaluation showed that the novel models developed and tested in FY24, while theoretically promising, provided only marginal improvements in comparability metrics over standard methods when applied to the real-world, noisy data. For instance, while a regression model controlling for assessor type slightly improved group separation, the results remained statistically weak and sensitive to outliers. No tested model achieved the target of consistently detecting expected differences with statistical significance (p \< 0.05) across multiple skills and program comparisons. The evaluation indicated these methods could not sufficiently overcome the underlying data quality and inconsistency issues within the current assessment paradigm.** **Experiment 4d (LLM Calibration): Evaluation showed mixed results. LLMs could group some obviously related rubric items with reasonable accuracy (\~70-75% agreement with experts for clear cases), but struggled significantly with nuanced distinctions or items requiring contextual understanding of the specific program's learning objectives. The accuracy achieved was deemed insufficient for reliable automated calibration across diverse rubrics at this stage. Using the LLM similarity scores to adjust normalisation in Exp 4c did not yield significant improvements in comparability.** **Overall Evaluation Conclusion for FY24 Normalisation Work: The systematic evaluation across these experiments demonstrated that the primary obstacle to reliable cross-program skill comparison was the high degree of uncontrolled variance and inconsistency originating from the assessment process itself (differing rubrics, assessor calibration issues). The normalisation methods attempted in FY24, both standard and novel, were insufficient to overcome this fundamental data quality challenge.** Evaluation of the methods developed to compare skill development is highly reliant on the quality and consistency of the assessment. In most cases, assessment data obtained was either through self- or peer assessment, resulting in inconsistencies and noisy data. Even application of the assessment criteria mentors/industry experts proved to be inconsistent, with no calibration between assessor groups within the same program. Consequently, direct comparison methods (through the use of matrices) failed to produce statistically significant results due to variations in program structures. Traditional normalisation methods also produced misleading comparisons when applied to diverse experiential learning datasets. It was established that a novel method is required. |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

| Work based learning (WBL) enables learners to develop skills in real world situations, allowing participants to demonstrate work-readiness, increasing their employability, improves retention and career progression. Feedback from industry professionals is one of the most impactful WBL experience, however, coordinating this feedback is time consuming and labour intensive, inherently limiting its scalability. Work simulations can provide a standardised version of WBL experiences. However, the feedback provided is usually broad, generalised and potentially unhelpful to participants’ development.  Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) offers the potential for immediate feedback to work submitted. This can help to keep participants engaged, which in turn could improve learning and course completion outcomes. However, the ability to generate meaningful feedback, to a defined quality standard is not known. Technical flaws and limitations of AI relevant to experiential learning, include: The inability to execute structured tasks requiring precision, such as working through a set rubric to reach a specified outcome. E.g., A rubric may define a grade based on the number of correctly identified items. However, depending on how the items are provided by the student and presented in the specimen answer/exemplar, the items may not be counted correctly, resulting in the wrong rubric being selected. Generalisation and/or or inaccurate feedback. Effective experiential learning requires student feedback to be accurate and specific to provide actionable outcomes. Susceptibility to ‘attacks’ resulting in successful extraction of exemplars and “answers” from the system, which is particularly problematic in the education context.  At the commencement of this activity, there were no known experiential learning feedback tools available which have successfully integrated this GenAI to achieve quality feedback outputs.   In undertaking this activity, Practera seeks to enhance the functionality of their work based, experiential, learning platform to provide real-time, quality feedback, which will enable new forms of career-connected learning experiences that can be scaled to meet demand, overcoming time and labour limitations due to the need for manual (human) feedback.  Practera hypothesises that new GenAI based experiential learning feedback functionality and tools will be capable of augmenting experiential learning by providing: Instant, high-quality feedback on the participants’ technical and professional skills Evaluating and enhancing feedback from industry professionals  Leading to continued and/or increased participant engagement, resulting in improved course completion outcomes. Sub-hypotheses guiding the progression of the activity include: Feedback provided by the new GenAI based tools will be: In the proper/correct context, overcoming any risk of AI hallucinations. Aligned with the learning rubrics.  Be of adequate quality and consistency, reflective of the input/answer (degree of detail, writing style etc.) provided (by the student) If successful, the outcome of this activity has the potential to remove the single biggest barrier to scale – cost of human engagement \- from the learning experience, while providing instant feedback. This could allow for feedback on work-based learning experiences to be completed in minutes to hours (depending on complexity). |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

| **Please describe any conclusions reached from your experiments.** |  |  |  |  | *4,000-character limit* |  |  |  |  |  |
| AI capability tests Experiments to test iterations of GPT-3.5 and GPT4 concluded that: The initial prototypes (both GPT-3.5 and 4 versions) were able to pick the correct weighted multiple-choice answer from the input rubric (e.g., if the overall answer was right/wrong, incomplete/complete/partially complete etc.) GPT-3.5 could provide feedback for answers/essays close in quality to the exemplar. When submissions were of poor of or middling quality, it tended to treat the submissions as if they were of high quality. Considering the range of possible qualities of student work, this effectively made effectively unusable for reviewing student assignments. This version was thus deemed unsuitable for AI marking. Finetuning GPT-3.5 Turbo on a curated set of Practera-specific assessments yielded a new model-based technique that outperformed the original GPT-3.5 version, although this showed promise for specific, high-volume assessment types, significant effort was required in data curation and management. The initial GPT-4’s ability to return quality feedback was highly dependent on the type of question and quality of the prompt. Initial tests used relatively high quality and consistent AI prompts. Adequate and useful feedback was provided for text and code-based questions with different levels of answer quality. Feedback was more erratic for list or instruction \-based answers, with totally wrong feedback being provided for the poorest answers.  Subsequent releases of GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o provided iterative improvements to feedback provided GPT-4 Turbo offered a better balance of performance, cost and features, although initial versions sometimes exhibited “laziness” or verbosity issues that required further prompt adjustments and monitoring Benchmarking of GPT-4o against GPT-4 for feedback quality and latency showed improved cost efficiency and speed while maintaining or exceeding the quality of GPT-4.  GPT-4o is now the chosen GenAI model used by Practera due to its proven capabilities and stability. It has also shown significant promise as a future optimisation path, pending further long-term evaluation of its consistency and reliability for specific feedback generation tasks.   GenAI feedback on student outcomes Student outcomes from the second study using GenAI generated feedback include: Statistically significant improvements in teamwork and conflict resolution abilities was reported, conflict resolution abilities increased from 4.1 to 4.5 on average. AI feedback was rated 4.3/5 in constructiveness and supporting iterative improvement AI feedback prompted students to reevaluate their problem-solving approaches, increasing critical thinking skills by 15%, scores on adaptability raised from 4.2 to 4.7 A 12 % increase in ability to effectively present innovative ideas was noted, with assessment scores improving from 3.9 to 4.4. AI feedback was rated 4.2/5 in helping students develop creative problem-solving skills and resilience AI based feedback assisted in enhancing students’ cultural awareness and adaptability, with students showing improvements in multiple key skills  AI based feedback was instrumental in guiding students through complex data-driven tasks, targeted feedback ensured understanding and application of key data science concepts, resulting in students showing notable skill improvements These modules received average engagement ratings of 4.3/5 and 89% of students recommending AI-based feedback learning. The immediacy and specificity of the feedback were cited as being critical to maintaining motivation and in fostering self-regulated learning, empowering students to take ownership of their skill development; supporting the hypothesis that AI generated feedback can improve course completion outcomes.  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
